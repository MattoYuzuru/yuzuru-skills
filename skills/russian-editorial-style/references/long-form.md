# Essays, articles, and explanatory long-form

## Identify the form

- **Explanation:** help the reader understand a mechanism or concept.
- **Analysis:** compare evidence and derive a bounded interpretation.
- **Argument:** defend a claim while addressing material objections.
- **Essay or column:** develop an authored perspective; structure may remain implicit.
- **Narrative:** organize experience in time, scene, and consequence.

Do not force every form into a hook, three arguments, and a call to action.

## Build coherence

For explanatory and analytical prose, establish the question or controlling
claim early enough to orient the reader. Order sections by dependency, chronology,
comparison, or argumentative function. Give each paragraph a job and maintain a
recognizable topic across its sentences.

Use headings when they expose genuine structure or support navigation. In a
short essay, paragraph transitions may do the work better. Make causal,
conditional, and contrastive relations explicit when readers could otherwise
infer the wrong relation.

## Evidence and stance

Keep evidence attached to the claim it supports. Preserve attribution, scope,
uncertainty, counterevidence, and distinctions between fact and inference. Do
not add balance mechanically; include a counterargument when it is material to
the question or supplied evidence.

## Voice

Retain the author's consequential choices: perspective, judgement, recurring
images, humour, restraint, and characteristic diction. Edit a striking phrase
only when it distorts meaning, genre, or audience fit. Do not add compulsory
metaphors, anecdotes, rhetorical questions, emotion, or first person.

After revision, check the thesis or question, evidence chain, qualifications,
and ending separately. An ending may resolve, complicate, or open the question;
it need not restate every section.
